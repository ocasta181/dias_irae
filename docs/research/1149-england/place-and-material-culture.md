# Place, provisions, and visual culture for autumn 1149

The campaign uses a fictional manor and parish in the district around [Lewes](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Lewes%2C+East+Sussex%2C+United+Kingdom). It follows the earlier town study's emphasis on relationships, Christian help, local pressure, and a community the player can save. The exact manor, mills, care house, armed refuge, and final food depot are new inventions. [Earlier town comparison](../1101-england/town-settings.md).

## A place selected for this story

**Rookmere** is the invented home holding. It is a modest manor with a hall, yard, parish, mill, gardens, grazing, and neighboring farms. It is not the historical earl's castle. Robert knows its people and owes them protection, while Alice has kept the household operating during his absence.

The historical town supplies a market, religious neighbors, estate interests, and a wider community. The countryside allows paths between farms, a stream crossing, a care house, a veterans' camp, and an abandoned fortified holding. Their precise locations and travel durations are not recovered medieval geography. The playable circuit is intentionally compressed; it is not a modern road map presented as a twelfth-century itinerary.

The setting's pressure is local. One agent exploits a vulnerable household and compromised food supply. Neither the whole county nor the whole kingdom is declared destroyed. The town can contain ordinary trade, worship, humor, and households helping strangers while the invented crisis grows nearby.

## Grain, scarcity, and the blight

The national archaeological synthesis finds significant regional crop variation and increasingly diverse cereal production. Its broad **c.1030–1220** phase includes the new date; it does not recover Rookmere's 1149 crops or stocks. Wheat is important in the southeast, and oat and rye cultivation also occur in the wider evidence. A landscape of nothing but rye would repeat an unsupported simplification. [Hamerow and colleagues, crop diversity and regional production](https://academic.oup.com/book/61548/chapter/537305187).

**Story choice.** Use wheat, barley, and other ordinary provisions in the wider visual world. The investigation follows a particular lot of rye that Father Hugh delivered with the casket and Hamon's men later mixed into relief supplies. Local stores are neither all poisoned nor all empty. Alice's separate stock and the care house's replacement supplies let the player achieve visible, durable relief.

Ergot provides a material reference for contaminated cereal and burning illness; no outbreak at this town in autumn 1149 is claimed. Impossible movement, voices, transformed creatures, and the casket's influence are supernatural fiction. The plot does not require victims to eat fungal pieces as a historically established religious remedy.

**Modern food-safety evidence, not characters' knowledge.** Ergot alkaloids can occur in several cereals, and visible fungal bodies are not a complete safety test: apparently clean samples can still contain them. The story's replacement food comes from a separate uncompromised supply, rather than a claim that picking out black pieces or baking proves safety. No doses or treatment protocol are supplied. [European Food Safety Authority, exposure assessment](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4902).

Separate seed grain, household food, dues, and relief supplies. Hamon's attempts to take seed and close access to a mill threaten the next year's life, not just the current ration. A sack, its route, witness accounts, and two conflicting records can establish the game's particular harm without reconstructing a complete medieval bureaucracy.

Bread, cooked grain, pulses, available vegetables, dairy, and household-specific meat or preserved food are conservative scene choices. Ale, water, and wine have different costs and uses; wine is not an absent substance in an English setting. Exact menus, prices, portions, and medicinal recipes remain unverified. The earlier provisions report supplies dated evidence, with its broad phases retained. [Earlier English provisions evidence](../1101-england/technology-and-provisions.md).

## Rank, names, and speech

Use an Anglo-Norman household within an English-speaking local community. **Robert, Alice, Walter, Hugh, Thomas, Anselm, Gilbert, Hamon, Ernald, Agatha, Godric, Oswin, Wulfstan, and Edith** are authorial, period-plausible name choices, not people recovered from a local 1149 charter. A name does not determine loyalty or language. The mixed household should contain personal histories rather than two modern national teams.

Dialogue is presented in readable modern English. Robert and Alice can move between household French and local English; Hugh and Thomas have clerical Latin; Oswin is learning to read. These individual competencies are fiction. Latin texts need translations or a credible reader, and differences in training can affect who controls a written claim. The earlier culture dossier dates the surviving language and manuscript evidence instead of reconstructing every person's speech. [Earlier English cultural evidence](../1101-england/culture.md).

## Buildings and the image of authority

The local Cluniac foundation has an older Romanesque building campaign and further work beginning around **1145**. This supports a living institution with work underway. It does not make its complete later church or later landholding current in 1149. [Keeper's building chronology](https://www.lewespriory.org.uk/history-overview).

Rookmere's hall, its parish of **Saint Peter**, the **House of Saint Michael**, and **Blackthorn Hold** are fictional. Use timber, plaster, thatch where appropriate, and selected masonry rather than making every dwelling a stone tower. Painted churches, worn sacred images, the sound of worship, and active charity belong in the living world. Building material does not rank the story's emotional importance.

Blackthorn Hold is a damaged fortified holding reused as an armed food depot. Its assumed license, tenure, and previous local damage are invented. No real castle is assigned an undocumented siege or a satanic foundation. The descent uses its store pits and a fictional impossible shaft; the old French shrine's Merovingian crypt and Roman mithraeum are not moved across the sea.

The later Isenheim imagery may remain an art reference for suffering and grotesque creatures. It is not a 1149 English object or a record of local care. Likewise, the title *Dias Irae* does not establish that the familiar complete chant is being sung in this parish. Music and images used as inspiration retain that distinction.

## Armor and recognizable orders

Mail, a practical helmet, shield, one-handed sword, wool, linen, leather, and repaired equipment remain the starting vocabulary. Fully enclosed great-helm forms develop later in the twelfth century, and a full plate harness is much later. A 1149 date therefore does not automatically validate every existing helmet concept. [Museum's equipment chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/arms-and-armor-in-medieval-europe).

The conservative historical reference is a nasal helmet and mail with a kite shield. The existing art brief records an author-approved full-face greathelm exception; preserve that deliberate choice when using its approved design, without calling it documented 1149 equipment. Specific padded layers, sleeve and hand construction, mail legs, and horse equipment need reference selection before final art. A worn personal or household mark is an artistic choice; it is not a claim to a securely dated hereditary coat of arms and full livery package.

Real military-order dress belongs to real members when introduced. The rogue fraternity uses an invented irregular badge and patched cloaks. The care house has work clothing and simple devotional signs, not a transported Antonine uniform. The active research distinguishes the usable Templar references from later Hospitaller war dress and heraldry.

## Season, calendar, and sky

Autumn into early winter provides harvest work, storage, shortening light, wet paths, and growing dependence on supplies. Rain at the prayer cross, damaged crops, and a blocked route are scene choices. No exact 1149 local weather series, famine, storm, or flood has been established here.

Keep medieval events in the **Julian calendar** when assigning dates. The draft uses relative seasonal timing rather than unverified weekdays or modern Gregorian equivalents. Its epilogue is in **1150**. The earlier 1101 eclipse calculations and weather examples do not establish events in this new season; no 1149 eclipse or conspicuous red Moon is asserted.
