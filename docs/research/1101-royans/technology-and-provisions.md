# Technology and daily provisions in 1101

The story runs from late summer into winter **1101**, through the [Royans](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Royans%2C+France), the [Isère valley](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Is%C3%A8re+valley%2C+France), and the [Drôme](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Dr%C3%B4me%2C+France) and [Rhône corridors](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Rh%C3%B4ne+valley%2C+France). These are modern geographical labels for territory then within the Kingdom of Burgundy-Arles and the Holy Roman Empire. “Dauphiné” is a convenient later regional reference, not a unified 1101 state. Rivoire and its household economy belong to the fiction.

The strongest material comparison is **Colletière**, on [Lake Paladru](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Lac+de+Paladru%2C+Is%C3%A8re%2C+France): an early eleventh-century farming settlement with an armed elite and unusually well-preserved objects. The department dates occupation to approximately **1006–1040**; the museum uses **1007 to about 1035**. Both make it an earlier regional comparison, not a Royans inventory for 1101. [Isère: excavation overview](https://culture.isere.fr/page/les-fouilles-archeologiques-du-site-de-colletiere); [Paladru museum: collections](https://www.museearcheologiquelacdepaladru.fr/le-malp/les-collections-exceptionnelles/lan-mil/).

**Documented evidence** applies to the stated place and date. **Regional analogy** uses an earlier or wider comparison. **Story inference** proposes a reconstruction without a surviving local record.

## Farming, harvest, and the winter reserve

**Regional analogy, securely excavated.** A Colletière excavation report identifies rye as the leading cereal, supplemented by wheat and oats, with less barley. Cereal husks show that threshing occurred at the settlement. The same report identifies pig export, although it does not establish the destination or scale of that trade. Thus a community can produce food for its own use and exchange some output, rather than living in complete economic isolation. [Michel Colardelle, excavation report, 1986, p. 155](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcme_0153-9337_1986_num_16_1_1177_t1_0155_0000_3).

Marie-Pierre Ruas's archaeological review of southern medieval [France](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=France) identifies wheat, barley, rye, oats, millets, fava beans, and peas. Rye appears more often at mountain sites in her sample. The evidence spans different centuries and cannot establish Rivoire's crop proportions. Seeds alone also do not reveal whether grain became bread, porridge, fodder, or malt. [Ruas, 1997, pp. 187, 190 and 195](https://www.persee.fr/doc/amime_0758-7708_1997_num_15_1_1323).

**Story inference.** Track food grain, seed retained for sowing, fodder, payments owed, and reserves separately. A household can have grain yet be unable to eat it without losing next year's crop or breaching an obligation. Track ownership too: the lord's store, the miller's intake, a tenant's seed, and charitable bread need not share one supply.

Ruas's corpus includes granaries, chests, and storage pits, with grain heating for drying, processing, or malt production. These are wider medieval comparisons. A pit's later rubbish does not automatically identify its original purpose or contents. [Ruas, pp. 182–184 and 190](https://www.persee.fr/doc/amime_0758-7708_1997_num_15_1_1323).

**Story inference.** A dry chamber with bins, sacks, or chests suits the fictional castrum. Damp, pests, fire, theft, and interrupted access threaten reserves. A cool underground room is not automatically suitable for grain. The invented Roman granary beneath the priory needs an explanation for its medieval drainage, ventilation, and reuse.

Harvest timing depends on crop, elevation, sowing season, and weather. No precise Royans calendar for 1101 was recovered. Ripe rye throughout late autumn needs an explanation, such as an unusually late crop or an abandoned harvest. Distinguish winter sowing and spring growth from grain already stored. For weather limits, use [the climate report](climate-and-sky.md).

## Mills, ovens, and control of food

Water-powered grain milling was an established technology by 1101. Archaeologist Virginie Serna describes the multiplication of mills from the tenth century, the shaping of small watercourses, and different arrangements suited to different rivers. Hand milling persisted alongside larger installations. Lordly rights could compel inhabitants to use a mill, oven, or press and pay a fee; a regional charter is still needed to prove the exact obligation for a given village. A cloth-fulling mill is an available eleventh-century comparison, not evidence for a mechanical ironworks in every valley. [Serna, *Typologie des moulins*, pp. 193–196](https://www.persee.fr/doc/sracf_1159-7151_2013_ant_43_1_1536).

Mills combined machinery with authority. Robert Philippe's study of the eleventh century between the [Seine](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Seine+river%2C+France) and [Loire](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Loire+river%2C+France) treats the mill as a place of social gathering and dispute, with ecclesiastical interests in the management of energy and work. This is a northern comparison; it does not prove identical rights in the Royans. [Philippe, *L'Église et l'énergie pendant le XIe siècle*, p. 107 onward](https://www.persee.fr/doc/shmes_1261-9078_1984_act_14_1_1405).

**Story inference.** A damaged channel, disputed fee, missing millstone, or fuel shortage can delay bread even when grain survives. Establish who controls the oven, whether households bring dough, and whether emergency hand grinding is permitted. These are local story arrangements, not universal rules.

Keep the stages separate: threshing frees grain; winnowing and sorting clean it; grinding produces flour; dough preparation and baking make bread. A miller's access to grain from several households makes the mill a credible place to observe changing supplies. It does not make the mill the origin of every illness. A common oven likewise links households socially without necessarily mixing their flour.

## Tools, crafts, iron, and fuel

Colletière supplies regional evidence for ards, sickles, hoes, axes, adzes, drills, a plane, textile shears, spindles, loom equipment, anvils, punches, forge waste, and leatherworking tools. An **ard** is a plough that opens the soil without the full turning action of a mouldboard plough. These finds show available skills and equipment, not a complete workshop in every home. [Isère: craft inventories](https://culture.isere.fr/page/les-fouilles-archeologiques-du-site-de-colletiere).

**Story inference.** Use wood for handles, tubs, bowls, carts, roof timbers, doors, and temporary structures; concentrate iron where strength, an edge, or a fastening is needed. Tool and strap repairs connect skilled work to survival. A smith able to maintain tools need not be able to manufacture a first-rate sword or mail shirt from ore.

Do not confuse **smelting**, which extracts metal from ore, with **smithing**, which works metal already produced. The excavated craft evidence does not identify an ore mine that supplied Rivoire in 1101. Nor does later regional mining prove an uninterrupted route to a particular village. Market iron, reused fittings, and metal brought through household connections are usable fictional supply choices; a named source mine needs separate documentation.

The canon's charcoal camps give fuel a producer and a transport problem. Their placement, ownership, and output remain inventions. Distinguish forge charcoal, hearth and oven firewood, and construction timber. Cutting, drying, hauling, and access rights can create shortages. Exact local fuel demand and woodland depletion in 1101 remain unknown.

**Story inference.** Show textile production and maintenance as repeated household tasks: spinning, weaving, washing, sewing, and patching. For fibers, dyes, clothing shapes, and the limits of later spinning-wheel imagery, use [the visual-culture report](music-and-visual-culture.md).

## Fortification and military equipment

The contemporary knight's equipment is not a plate harness. The Metropolitan Museum's survey uses Saint Anselm, who lived **about 1033–1109**, for a list including horse, bridle, saddle, spurs, hauberk, helmet, shield, lance, and sword. It dates more enclosing, face-covering helmets to the **late twelfth century**, and complete plate armor to approximately **1420**. These later forms must not define a returning crusader in 1101. [Metropolitan Museum, *Arms and Armor in Medieval Europe*](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/arms-and-armor-in-medieval-europe).

A **hauberk** is a mail shirt; **mail** consists of linked metal rings. A pointed helmet with a **nasal**, a bar protecting the nose, is supported by eleventh-century imagery. Dominic Tweddle traces this form through dated coins, manuscripts, and the [Bayeux Tapestry](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Mus%C3%A9e+de+la+Tapisserie+de+Bayeux%2C+France). The long, tapering **kite shield** is another appropriate period reference. Neither establishes a local inventory. [Tweddle, p. 1103](https://www.collections.yorkarchaeologicaltrust.co.uk/files/original/74497/GB2837-PUB-AY-17-08_Anglian-Helmet-Coppergate_D_Tweddle_Inc-Unbound-Illustrations.pdf); [English Heritage: weaponry of 1066](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/histories/1066-and-the-norman-conquest/the-weaponry-of-1066/).

**Evidence limits for the canon.** This review has not verified integrated mail mittens, or *mufflers*, as standard Royans equipment in 1101. Nor has it established that every mail wearer used the same thick quilted gambeson, or that every poor fighter had one. Cloth beneath mail is a reasonable reconstruction; its exact construction and universal use need stronger dated evidence. The game's damage model is a design choice, not a documented medieval performance test. Full hereditary heraldic surcoats and standardized household livery also require the date distinctions in [the houses report](noble-houses.md).

Crossbows belong in the available technology. Colletière has bolts and parts of crossbows, including a surviving wooden trigger arrangement. Its saddle and harness finds also support developed riding equipment well before the story. These finds should not be illustrated with the later steel bow and geared spanning mechanisms familiar from late medieval collections. [Paladru museum: saddle and crossbows](https://www.museearcheologiquelacdepaladru.fr/le-malp/les-collections-exceptionnelles/lan-mil/).

**Story inference.** The fictional stone hall and tower can coexist with timber buildings and palisades. Avoid making every fortified household an elaborate later castle. Repairs, watch arrangements, defensible doors, stored missiles, fodder, and access to water often matter more to a small garrison scene than the height of its tower. Detailed architectural choices belong in the visual-culture report.

## Transport, crossings, and resource origins

The region has genuine early bridge evidence. The municipal heritage guide for [Romans-sur-Isère](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Romans-sur-Is%C3%A8re%2C+France) dates the first mention of its Isère bridge to **1033**, with toll rights held by the canons of Saint-Barnard. Its fortified gateway dates to **1393**. This supports a crossing and toll conflict in 1101, but does not establish the precise construction of the bridge at that date. The surviving bridge and later urban quays cannot simply be copied backward. [Romans, historic-center guide, section “Pont Vieux”](https://www.ville-romans.fr/application/files/3815/4963/1299/Guide_centre_historique.pdf).

The canon's stone bridge with chapels at [Pont-en-Royans](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Pont-en-Royans%2C+France) also needs a date check. This research has not verified that construction for 1101; retain its plan as fiction pending local evidence.

Water transport also has a regional archaeological example: Colletière's dugout, made from an oak trunk, could serve fishing and movement of goods across the lake. It is not a model for all Rhône cargo boats. Lake craft, river craft, ferries, and seagoing ships solve different problems. [Paladru museum: dugout](https://www.museearcheologiquelacdepaladru.fr/le-malp/les-collections-exceptionnelles/lan-mil/).

**Story inference.** Use pack animals on difficult paths, carts where surfaces permit, and boats for selected bulky loads. No exact journey speed, cart capacity, or navigable route into Rivoire has been established. Guarin's mule also consumes fodder and needs rest. Give bridges, ferries, and fords distinct practical conditions.

| Resource | Defensible origin or producer | Limit for the story |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary grain and pulses | Farms and estate stores. | Name a fictional source for each important lot; no local 1101 ledger verified. |
| Meat, dairy, leather, wool | Livestock producers and craft labor; pigs, cattle, sheep, and fishing equipment occur at Colletière. | No fixed local ration established. [Isère inventory](https://culture.isere.fr/page/les-fouilles-archeologiques-du-site-de-colletiere). |
| Timber and fuel | Nearby woodland and charcoal makers are conservative fictional sources. | Exact rights, sites, and quantities remain unknown. |
| Iron | A smith works supplied or reused metal. | No named 1101 mine-to-Rivoire connection verified. |
| Salt | Established Mediterranean salt production provides a possible external source. | Production evidence is stronger than evidence for its exact route into the Royans. |
| Wine | Cultivation and wine production belong to the wider food economy. | No particular vineyard, vintage, or appellation established. |
| Rare imports | Merchant networks. | Mediterranean availability does not prove local availability. |

Salt was essential to food preparation and preservation, livestock, and leatherworking. André Dupont's charter-based study documents [Narbonne](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Narbonne%2C+France) saltworks before 1101, including a **1048** grant and **1080** arrangements involving salt tithes. His evidence also warns against treating early production as the fully developed later export industry. A Mediterranean origin for salt purchased in the story is plausible; a regular 1101 convoy from a named saltwork to the Royans remains unverified. Avoid importing the later royal salt-tax system into this setting. [Dupont, *L'exploitation du sel sur les étangs de Languedoc*, especially pp. 7–12](https://www.persee.fr/doc/anami_0003-4398_1958_num_70_41_6196).

## Food by household, season, and religious rule

A plausible ordinary meal uses grain as bread or a cooked cereal preparation, with pulses and whatever garden or preserved food the household has. This is a **suggested menu**, not a recovered Royans recipe. Better bread, more frequent animal foods, a varied supply, and privileged access to reserves can distinguish the castrum from a poor household. A tenant need not eat meat every day; neither should the entire countryside be described as vegetarian.

The older *Capitulare de villis*, an instruction for royal estate management, lists garden crops and fruits including fava beans, peas, leeks, onions, garlic, cabbage, apples, pears, and nuts. It also names cheese, butter, salted or dried meats, vinegar, malt, honey, wax, and fermented drinks. The text predates 1101 by centuries and is prescriptive. It establishes a long-standing repertoire, not the presence of every listed crop or product in every local garden. Its vocabulary must also be read carefully: medieval plant names do not always match modern species. [*Capitulare de villis*, chapters 34 and 70, text and translation](https://plantuse.plantnet.org/fr/Texte_du_Capitulare_de_villis).

Green vegetables can be underrepresented in seed evidence because people eat other plant parts. Hearths, latrines, and stores also preserve different evidence. One dense cereal deposit does not describe an entire diet. [Ruas, pp. 182–187](https://www.persee.fr/doc/amime_0758-7708_1997_num_15_1_1323).

**Story inference.** Late summer supplies fresh produce; autumn brings fruit handling and preservation; winter depends increasingly on stores. This is a scene framework, not a reconstructed calendar. Slaughter decisions depend on breeding stock, working animals, milk, fodder, and preservation materials. Cheese, dried pulses, nuts, and preserved meat offer different storage options; local recipes remain unspecified.

Fasting changes both ingredients and meal times. The **Rule of Benedict** provides a dated older normative example: cooked dishes, bread, possible fruit or vegetables, restrictions on meat from four-footed animals, wine allowances, and adjustments for labor, heat, sickness, age, and season. Its Wednesday and Friday summer fasts are a monastic rule, not a complete calendar for every lay household. During Lent, serving frequency and permitted foods must match the chosen community and its dispensations. Do not make one universal rule for fish, eggs, and dairy without checking it. [Rule, chapters 37–41](https://www.archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbemjo2.html).

**Story inference.** Journey food can include bread, cheese, pulses to cook at a stop, or preserved meat when permitted. Dried grain and pulses require water, a vessel, fuel, and time; they are not automatically an immediate meal.

Honey is the conservative everyday sweetener to place in a household with access to it; the estate text also pairs it with wax production. Sugar is not wholly unknown to the medieval Mediterranean. Petros Bouras-Vallianatos documents sugar manufacture in the Islamic world and sugar-based medicines reaching Byzantine practice by the late eleventh or early twelfth century. This supports the possibility that a traveled crusader has encountered it. It does **not** establish sugar in a Royans pantry or an early Antonine recipe. [*Capitulare*, chapter 34](https://plantuse.plantnet.org/fr/Texte_du_Capitulare_de_villis); [Bouras-Vallianatos, 2021, sections 1 and 3.1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7613226/).

Jean-Charles Ducène's review identifies pepper among aromatics imported through maritime trade from Southeast Asia. It places a new import corridor through the [Red Sea](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Red+Sea) and [Egypt](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Egypt) from the beginning of the eleventh century, with Egypt's Mediterranean redistribution role growing further in the twelfth. This establishes wider exchange networks; the abstract does not establish spice availability, quantities, or prices in the Royans in 1101. [Ducène, “Medieval Spice Trade in the Arabic World,” *Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Food Studies*, 11 December 2024; abstract only](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61795/chapter/546044527).

Modern protected names for cheeses, wines, or walnuts do not prove the same named product existed in 1101. Exclude potatoes, tomatoes, maize, cacao, American *Capsicum* peppers, and the American common bean from a historically grounded local menu. Coffee and tea should not appear as ordinary Royans beverages. This is an editorial exclusion list; the fictional supernatural world can depart from it deliberately.

## Water, wine, cervoise, and the cider question

People drank water. “Everyone drank beer because all water was unsafe” is false. Katherine Harvey cites Wulfstan of [Worcester](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Worcester%2C+England), who died in **1095**, choosing water for ascetic reasons. Her detailed boiling advice and urban water systems are mostly later examples, not a 1101 village sanitation plan. [Wellcome Collection, Harvey](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/the-vilest-of-all-beverages).

Wine has an established place in the Benedictine rule, which recognizes that local availability differs. The older estate instruction also lists **cervoise**, a grain-based fermented drink, and mead. These support a wider repertoire of beverages, without identifying their relative consumption in the Royans. Avoid giving every settlement the same beer-centered drinking culture or assuming a particular modern beer style. [Rule, chapter 40](https://www.archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbemjo2.html); [*Capitulare*, chapter 34](https://plantuse.plantnet.org/fr/Texte_du_Capitulare_de_villis).

**Unverified local detail.** Apples do not by themselves establish a regional cider industry. This review found no securely dated evidence for Royans cider production in 1101. The cider in the castrum undercroft can remain a fictional household preparation; describe its quantity and provenance as part of that household rather than as the region's standard drink. Similarly, the condition of last year's apples needs a specific storage explanation rather than an assumption that fruit remains fresh indefinitely.

## Writing, learning, and care

Written knowledge travels through particular people and institutions. A book is a hand-produced object; parchment is prepared animal skin. Books, writing tablets, pens, inventories, and a cellarer's management responsibilities already appear in the Rule of Benedict. This supports practical written administration as well as religious learning. It does not imply that every lord or tenant can read, or that a rural priory possesses every important medical text. [Rule, chapters 31–33](https://www.archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbemjo1.html).

Paper must be dated regionally. The manuscript **Silos 6**, associated with [Santo Domingo de Silos](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Monasterio+de+Santo+Domingo+de+Silos%2C+Spain), includes an eleventh-century paper section alongside parchment. A recent study considers the paper likely imported from the Islamic world and rejects an unsupported dating to 950. Thus “Europe had no paper” is too broad. An ordinary paper account book in the Royans in 1101 is nonetheless unverified; parchment and tablets are conservative choices. [Cambridge study of Silos 6, section “The manuscript” and notes 18–22](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-music-history/article/scribal-identity-and-scribal-roles-in-early-medieval-iberia-a-case-study-of-santo-domingo-de-silos-biblioteca-del-monasterio-ms-6/0AD896BDC49EA3C5F5127A9910322BD0).

Learned medicine was changing. In the late eleventh century, Constantine the African translated Arabic medical works into Latin in the southern Italian environment of [Salerno](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Salerno%2C+Italy) and [Monte Cassino](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Abbazia+di+Montecassino%2C+Italy). Greek, Arabic, and Latin traditions interacted through these centers. The transmission was active before 1101; their later university curriculum was not already universal. Guarin can plausibly have encountered a different medical culture on his travels, but a specific book or physician at La Motte needs an explicit fictional connection. [National Library of Medicine: crossroads of medical knowledge](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/odysseyofknowledge/crossroad.html); [later teaching development](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/odysseyofknowledge/translationtoteaching.html).

Care can be practical and devotional together. Benedict's rule prescribes a separate place and an attendant for the sick, baths as needed, and meat for severely weakened patients. These are older monastic norms, not records of the exact charitable house at [La Motte, now Saint-Antoine-l'Abbaye](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Saint-Antoine-l%27Abbaye%2C+Is%C3%A8re%2C+France), in 1101. [Rule, chapter 36](https://www.archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbemjo1.html).

**Later practice and unverified chronology.** Herbal wine, *Saint Vinage*, pork-fat salves, institutional amputation practice, and particular prostheses cannot all be treated as a documented 1101 treatment protocol. The orders report identifies the early alms confraternity and later institutional development. The canon's detailed hospital treatments can remain deliberate inventions. Improvement after admission can also follow a change of food supply; it need not demonstrate that a particular salve removed the poison. [Antonine evidence and recipe limits](christian-orders.md#antonines-the-strongest-local-connection).

## Ergot: connect the plot to food batches

**Modern explanation, not characters' knowledge.** Ergot alkaloids cause poisoning when contaminated cereal products are consumed. Rye is especially relevant, but other cereals can also carry contamination. Affected people do not transmit ergotism to others through touch or proximity. Sorting out visible fungal bodies can reduce exposure; an apparently clean batch can still contain alkaloids. Processing and baking have variable effects and must not be treated as certain detoxification. [European Food Safety Authority, 2017 exposure assessment](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4902).

In the plant disease cycle, infection concerns cereal flowers, with infected ovaries later developing into the dark fungal bodies that can contaminate the harvest. Autumn rain on already harvested grain is not the same event as infection of a growing cereal at flowering. Stored grain can carry a previous season's contamination into a later meal. [American Phytopathological Society, *Ergot*, disease-cycle section](https://www.apsnet.org/edcenter/pdlessons/Pages/Ergot.aspx).

**Story inference.** Identify each suspect batch and its path: field, harvest, store, sale or levy, mill, flour, bread, eater. If several households fall ill together, decide whether they share grain, flour, or bread. Agnes eating mill sweepings is a credible route of exposure if contaminated material is actually present. A patient sleeping beside another patient does not acquire the toxin from that neighbor. Repeated illness after an apparent cure needs renewed exposure, lingering injury, another disease, or the story's supernatural cause. Distinguish these mechanisms when planning clues.

## What is established, growing, or later?

| Status in 1101 | Relevant material culture | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Established** | Hand tools, timber construction, smithing, textile and leather work, cereal stores, water mills, mail, nasal helmets, shields, horses, and crossbows. | Everyday competence can coexist with scarcity and limited access to expensive equipment. |
| **Growing, with regional variation** | Mill development and associated rights; exchange of farm products; new Latin medical translations; some paper use outside the region. | Let innovations arrive through identifiable people and networks. Do not make them universal. |
| **Continuing alongside change** | Hand grinding, household craft, and older medical texts. | New methods do not erase old ones overnight. |
| **No local decline established** | No technological or dietary collapse documented for Royans in 1101. | Story shortages remain fictional events. |
| **Later or unverified for this setting** | Full plate harness, enclosing great helms, standardized heraldic uniforms, universal mail mittens and gambesons, routine village paper ledgers, familiar later Antonine treatment systems, and a documented local cider industry. | Record these as intentional inventions or replace them with the dated references above. |

The main unresolved limits are local crop ratios, harvest dates, mine and fuel origins, commercial quantities, riverboat forms, cider production, and the early charitable house's dispensary. Keep these story choices distinct from the dated evidence above.
