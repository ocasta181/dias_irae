# Music and visual culture around 1101

The story runs from late summer into winter **1101** in the [Royans](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Royans%2C+France), then along the [Isère valley](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Is%C3%A8re+valley%2C+France) and the [Drôme](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Dr%C3%B4me%2C+France) and [Rhône corridor](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Rh%C3%B4ne+valley%2C+France). Modern regional names locate the campaign; they do not supply its medieval political boundaries. The fictional buildings and characters come from the [location dossiers](../../../story/locations.md) and [character dossiers](../../../story/characters.md).

The best reconstruction combines **local material evidence**, **dated comparisons from elsewhere**, and **explicit story inference**. An object made before 1101 proves that a technique or form existed. It does not prove that every local household used it. A surviving twelfth-century manuscript can preserve older words, but its copying date does not by itself date their composition or local performance.

## Music: prayer, memory, performance

### The liturgical sound of the day

**Established in 1101.** Latin chant is the strongest basis for the shrine's sound. The Benedictine Rule describes an Office assembled from psalms, hymns, lessons, responses and canticles, with prayer at night and at named daytime hours. Chapter 18 requires the whole 150-psalm Psalter each week, while permitting a different distribution. This is an organized cycle of worship, not an uninterrupted funeral chant. [Rule of Benedict, chapters 17–20](https://archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbefjo3.html).

**Story inference.** Give different spaces different voices: a cantor beginning a phrase, the community answering, a priest reciting, a novice rehearsing, and patients hearing worship through a doorway. Bells can punctuate this pattern. A small priory can have a small vocal force. An exhausted brother missing a response conveys institutional strain. The exact antiphoner and customary of [La Motte, now Saint-Antoine-l'Abbaye](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Saint-Antoine-l%27Abbaye%2C+Is%C3%A8re%2C+France), in 1101 have not been identified here.

Sequences, chants associated with particular feasts, were already a developed genre. Margot Fassler traces them from the ninth century and identifies *Victimae paschali laudes*, the Easter sequence, as widespread in the late eleventh century. This gives the sound world joy, celebration and seasonal variation as well as penitence. It does not place an Easter performance in an autumn scene. [Fassler, “Women and Their Sequences,” introductory discussion](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/704043).

### What a singer's book can look like

**Current and developing.** Neumes are musical signs that indicate melodic movement; their precision varies with the notation system. They should not be drawn as a modern score. A northern French manuscript dated **1025–1099**, British Library Additional Manuscript 17808, contains Guido of Arezzo's music treatises, musical bars with neumes, and earlier teaching on polyphony. It is direct evidence that eleventh-century French readers could encounter these materials. Guido's pitch-teaching and notation reforms are therefore available to the period, although their adoption at this particular shrine remains unproved. [British Library manuscript catalogue](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/032-002028268).

Learning by ear and using a book are compatible. **Story inference:** an experienced singer can correct Odo from memory while a written sign helps him recall the contour. Avoid assuming that all singers read equally well, that every book has the same notation, or that a modern recording preserves the precise speed and vocal manner of 1101.

Polyphony, music with more than one voice part, also predates the story. The **Winchester Troper, about 1020–1030**, contains early liturgical polyphony from [Winchester](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Winchester%2C+England). Early *organum*, adding another voice to chant, is possible within the wider period. That English witness does not document its use in a Royans parish. [Corpus Christi College, “Listening to the Library,” manuscript 473](https://www.corpus.cam.ac.uk/parker-library/collections/exhibitions/listening-library-matthew-parkers-sonorous-books).

The elaborate three- and four-part organum associated with [Notre-Dame in Paris](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Cath%C3%A9drale+Notre-Dame+de+Paris%2C+France) belongs later. Eric Nemarich places the cathedral's transformative polyphonic period from the **second half of the twelfth century** into the thirteenth. It must not become the shrine's ordinary 1101 music. Precise mensural notation, in which note shapes encode rhythmic values, develops later; the University of Basel's teaching example is late thirteenth century. The *Ars nova* reforms associated with Philippe de Vitry belong to about **1320**. [Nemarich, dated research abstract](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/727887); [Guillaume Gross, abstract on three- and four-part organum](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/plainsong-and-medieval-music/article/abs/organum-at-notredame-in-the-twelfth-and-thirteenth-centuries-rhetoric-in-words-and-music/FD144127BE1648E07AEAD3B074986472); [Basel, early mensural notation](https://tales.nmc.unibas.ch/en/from-ink-to-sound-32/early-mensural-notation-190/preparing-a-transcription-972); [Tatsuo Minagawa, *Ars nova* research abstract](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/bigaku/25/3/25_KJ00003902284/_article/-char/en).

### *Dies irae*: distinguish the familiar song from older language

The title and musical inspiration can remain deliberate artistic choices. The complete familiar *Dies irae* Requiem sequence is a later reference: Fassler places it in the late thirteenth century. This research does not establish its full text and familiar melody as a song available at La Motte in 1101. Earlier textual roots should not be treated as proof that the later sequence already existed in complete form. [Fassler, discussion of the Requiem sequence](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/704043).

There is, however, a useful **period funeral text**. The Hartker Antiphoner from [Saint-Gall](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Abbey+of+Saint+Gall%2C+Switzerland), dated to the late tenth century, includes *Libera me domine de morte aeterna*. Its entry on **page 198** has a verse beginning *Dies illa dies irae*. The Cantus catalogue distinguishes later additions elsewhere in the volume and places the Office of the Dead in the original layer beginning on page 196. Fear of judgment and a “day of wrath” thus have securely earlier liturgical expression. This is a dated monastic comparison, not a recovered local performance. [Cantus chant inventory](https://cantusdatabase.org/chant/460487/); [manuscript description and layers](https://cantusdatabase.org/source/123718/).

**Story inference.** Use that older devotional vocabulary for a brother's spoken prayer or a funeral. Commission a researched reconstruction if an exact historical chant is needed. A later *Dies irae* quotation in the external score remains a separate creative decision.

### Secular songs and stories

**Emerging, with a local evidence limit.** William IX, **1071–1127**, was duke of [Aquitaine](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Aquitaine%2C+France) and count of [Poitou](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Poitou%2C+France), and is the earliest troubadour whose poems survive. The early Occitan lyric tradition belongs close to the story's date. Its later flowering must not be assigned wholesale to 1101. Neither William's lifetime nor his status establishes that a particular surviving song was already performed in the Royans that year. [French national library authority record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb11886486f); [Toulouse municipal library exhibition on early troubadours](https://expo.rosalis.bibliotheque.toulouse.fr/troubadours-langue-d-oc-et-jeux-floraux/chapitre/aux-origines-de-lacademie-des-jeux-floraux-moyen-age-troubadours-langue-doc-et-consistoire-du-gai-savoir/).

A household song need not be a polished declaration of courtly love. **Story inference:** jokes, boasts, complaints, remembered refrains and a performer's account of a lord's deeds are useful choices. Write new material as invention. Do not give every villager a later troubadour repertoire or modern standard French pronunciation. The precise local speech and musical dialect need a separate linguistic study.

Heroic narrative provides another model. The famous [Oxford](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Oxford%2C+England) witness to the *Chanson de Roland*, manuscript Digby 23, is catalogued as **twelfth century, possibly its second quarter**, in Anglo-Norman. Oral transmission and earlier composition are separate questions; this witness alone cannot settle them. A returning crusader can tell heroic stories as a plausible reconstruction, but quoting the whole surviving version as a known 1101 local song goes beyond this evidence. [Biblissima record carrying Bodleian manuscript metadata](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/0613c4a0e39539c7e29301f2e529e962b8e771bd).

The poem's invented gods of its Muslim opponents belong to literary polemic. They do not document real Islamic worship; see the [religions and rumors report](religions-and-rumors.md).

### Instruments: the unusually strong nearby evidence

The early eleventh-century settlement at [Colletière, Lake Paladru](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Colleti%C3%A8re%2C+Lac+de+Paladru%2C+France) gives a nearby material comparison, several generations before 1101. Its surviving bird-bone pipe has **three finger holes**. The museum considers the bone probably vulture and describes a missing block at the mouthpiece. A modern specialist made it sound by adapting his playing technique; that experiment does not prove that its original player used it as a transverse flute. A one-handed pipe is a proposed reconstruction. Its original tune is lost. [Paladru museum, instrument study](https://www.museearcheologiquelacdepaladru.fr/et-soudain-on-entendit-la-musique-de-lan-mil/).

Colletière also yielded a **muse and a vièle**, identified as pipe and fiddle remains. [Departmental excavation summary](https://culture.isere.fr/page/les-fouilles-archeologiques-du-site-de-colletiere).

The Tiberius Psalter, made in the third quarter of the eleventh century, depicts David with a harp and diagrams of instruments including a bell, psaltery and percussion. These are useful dated visual references for plucked strings, bells and drums. Biblical images are not an inventory of a local band, and the catalogue's instrument names should not be converted into standard modern designs. [British Library, Tiberius Psalter](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-001102251).

**Later comparison.** The celebrated organistrum at [Santiago de Compostela's cathedral](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Catedral+de+Santiago+de+Compostela%2C+Spain) belongs to its later sculptural program: the Pórtico de la Gloria lintels are dated **1188**. Modern replicas of the represented instruments are valuable experiments, not 1101 local evidence. This establishes the date of that reference, not an absolute invention date for every wheel instrument. [Cathedral instrument reconstruction](https://catedraldesantiago.es/el-museo-catedral-de-santiago-expone-replicas-de-los-instrumentos-musicales-del-portico-de-la-gloria/); [dated architectural history](https://catedraldesantiago.es/en/cathedral/).

Reserve the modern violin family, formed around **1550**, the piano, invented around **1700**, and the accordion, patented in **1829**, for the external score. A medieval fiddle is a different design reference. [Metropolitan Museum, violin history](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/violin-makers-nicolo-amati-1596-1684-and-antonio-stradivari-1644-1737); [Metropolitan Museum, piano history](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-piano-the-pianofortes-of-bartolomeo-cristofori-1655-1731); [transcribed accordion patent](https://concertina.org/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Demian.pdf).

## Art: painted surfaces and precious objects

### Walls, images and religious space

Painted churches are a firm period choice. At [Saint-Savin-sur-Gartempe](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Abbaye+de+Saint-Savin-sur-Gartempe%2C+France), the principal paintings date from the **late eleventh to early twelfth century**. Genesis and Exodus occupy the nave vault; an Apocalypse cycle occupies the entrance porch; the crypt tells the martyrs' story. This is a large monastery outside the campaign region, so its scale should not be copied into every village church. It establishes that extensive narrative painting belongs to the period. [Abbey chronology](https://www.abbaye-saint-savin.fr/fr/un-site-du-patrimoine-mondial-de-lunesco); [description of the painted cycles](https://www.abbaye-saint-savin.fr/fr/des-peintures-murales-uniques-au-monde).

Another close-date comparison is the *Virgin and Child in Majesty and the Adoration of the Magi*, **about 1100**, from [Tredòs](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Tred%C3%B2s%2C+Val+d%27Aran%2C+Spain). The enthroned holy figure gives a period reference for sacred authority. It is a Catalan example, not evidence that the fictional Saint-Laurent had this exact composition. [Metropolitan Museum, object record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/472381).

**Story inference.** The canon's limewash, painted apse and repaired plaster can make a living sacred interior. Let the horror damage or distort a maintained image. Smoke, damp, repainting and unequal resources can produce variation between buildings. Avoid making all churches uniformly bare stone because paint has disappeared from modern photographs. Specific pigments and restoration layers require object-level checks before production.

### Books, Apocalypse and portable wealth

The **Saint-Sever Beatus, about 1060**, is a particularly useful contemporary visual reference. Made at [Saint-Sever](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Abbaye+de+Saint-Sever%2C+Landes%2C+France), it combines the Apocalypse with Beatus of Liébana's older commentary. Abbot Grégoire de Montaner commissioned its decorative cycle from three artists. Red and brown inks distinguish Scripture from commentary. The book shows how an eleventh-century monastery could turn visions into a sustained visual explanation. It does not prove that this manuscript or a copy reached La Motte. [French national library, manuscript study](https://www.bnf.fr/fr/le-beatus-de-saint-sever).

**Story inference.** Use it alongside period manuscripts when designing Revelation's beasts, book pages and visionary images. Odo's familiarity with Scripture can differ from a lay viewer's recognition of pictures. The fictional illuminator's prophecy remains invention; no local workshop or surviving 1101 Anthony cycle was identified.

A tenth-century ivory Crucifixion from [Constantinople](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Istanbul%2C+Turkey), mounted in a **late eleventh-century Spanish** setting, provides a concrete model for reused precious material. Its setting combines gilded silver, wood, stones and glass, including a seal inscribed in Arabic. The proposed association with Queen Felicia of [Aragon](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Aragon%2C+Spain) is uncertain. The object's parts have different dates and origins. [Metropolitan Museum, ivory and setting](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/464015).

**Story inference.** A reliquary or book cover can have a history longer than its current owner. Foreign workmanship or an Arabic inscription alone does not reveal a hidden cult. This comparison supports layered objects and exchange, not the provenance of Fulk's fictional box.

### Embroidery and color

The eleventh-century embroidery associated with [Bayeux](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Bayeux%2C+France) uses colored wool on linen; it is embroidery rather than a woven tapestry. The museum identifies woad for blue, madder for several warm shades, and weld for yellow. It also describes several stitches. This is strong evidence for skilled needlework and a varied color range, although its depicted garment colors are not a precise catalogue of everyone's clothing dyes. [Bayeux Museum, materials and technique](https://www.bayeuxmuseum.com/en/the-bayeux-tapestry/discover-the-bayeux-tapestry/tapestry-or-embroidery/).

**Story inference.** A border worked around a collar, a carefully repaired cloth or an exceptional altar textile can communicate labor and status. Broad color availability does not establish fixed heraldic uniforms. Keep family colors and later order badges subject to the dates in the [noble-house](noble-houses.md) and [Christian-order](christian-orders.md) reports.

### Isenheim remains the intentional later inspiration

The Isenheim Altarpiece, made **1512–1516** for the Antonine establishment at [Issenheim](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Issenheim%2C+France), combines Grünewald's paintings with Nicolas of Haguenau's sculpture. Its Crucifixion, Anthony imagery and diseased bodies make it a useful artistic source for this story. [Unterlinden Museum, official object guide](https://www.musee-unterlinden.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/160129_UNTER_FICHES_11.pdf).

The canon expressly chooses this later work as its spiritual art reference. Preserve that choice. Its emotional force can guide the horror, while period evidence guides the objects inside the scene. Its elaborate altarpiece form, depicted costumes and medical imagery cannot themselves date a 1101 ward or church furnishing.

## Clothing: useful layers, visible status, individual variation

### The working reconstruction

The Saint-Antoine museum's clothing chronology describes an established western base of a **T-shaped tunic**, added triangular pieces for movement, belt, cloak, stockings or hose, and longer women's garments with dressed hair generally covered by a veil. Its textile study identifies **linen next to the body and wool over it**, with silk available to elites. These are broad historical patterns, not a surviving Royans wardrobe. The same chronology places widespread long noble clothing in the **twelfth century** and the extravagant houppelande in the late fourteenth. [Museum clothing dossier, Sophie Jolivet, pp. 6–7; textile study, p. 11](https://musees.isere.fr/sites/portail-musee-fr/files/inline-files/DP_MSA_SUR%20UN%20FIL_2024_0.pdf#page=6).

**Story inference.** Build ordinary figures from these layers. Match sleeves and hems to farm work or riding. Distinguish people through repairs, fit and cloth quality. Winter scenes can add heavier layers and head coverings; the [climate report](climate-and-sky.md) explains the limits on exact weather.

The name *bliaut* or *bliaud* appears in broad costume histories spanning the **eleventh to mid-fourteenth centuries**. Such labels do not date a particular fitted silhouette, trailing sleeve or lacing system to 1101. The Ministry of Culture's overview groups several centuries and uses later illustrations. Use it for the sequence of changes, not as a precise costume pattern for Almodis. [Ministry of Culture, clothing chronology](https://histoiredesarts.culture.gouv.fr/Fiches-reperes/Mode).

**Story inference.** Almodis can have a longer, better-finished outer garment, a veil and an inherited decorative fastening. Guarin's riding clothes can prioritize movement, with a better cloak marking rank. Neither needs a complete later court costume. Record any chosen family palette as fiction unless a dated source establishes it. Use surviving images to check the reference's date and purpose before copying their folds and sleeves.

### Shoes and small objects: regional evidence

Colletière preserves shoes and leatherworking evidence. Cutting waste shows hides worked on site; the museum identifies livestock leathers, oak bark and imported Mediterranean sumac as tanning materials. Tools include cutting patterns, awls and lacing implements; a small maple last was used for very young children's footwear. Different models and sewing methods indicate craft choices. This earlier settlement gives a stronger local comparison than a generic image of barefoot peasants. [Paladru museum, shoemaking study](https://www.museearcheologiquelacdepaladru.fr/dans-les-pas-des-artisans-de-lan-mil/).

Its collections also include boxwood combs and brass or tin pins and fasteners, sometimes decorative. These make grooming and small ornament part of the regional material picture. [Paladru museum, early medieval collections](https://www.museearcheologiquelacdepaladru.fr/le-malp/les-collections-exceptionnelles/lan-mil/).

**Story inference.** Give shoes shape, stitching and wear; give a pin or comb a use. Poverty, displacement and illness can remove these possessions during the campaign. Their absence can then tell a story, rather than serving as the assumed condition of every medieval person.

### Monks and hospital brothers

Chapter 55 of the Benedictine Rule makes clothing responsive to climate and season. It specifies a tunic, cowl, work garment and footwear, provides replacement garments for washing, and tells monks to accept locally obtainable cloth without concern for its color. A prescribed monastic wardrobe therefore does not prove that every eleventh-century community had identical black garments. [Rule of Benedict, chapter 55](https://archive.osb.org/rb/text/rbeaad1.html).

This rule concerns monks; it does not establish the first hospital brothers' precise costume. The familiar Antonine black habit and blue tau have their own dating limits in the [orders report](christian-orders.md#antonines-the-strongest-local-connection). The canon's extensive tau program remains an explicit visual choice.

## Architecture: active Romanesque building, older fabric, later additions

### The style available in 1101

Romanesque is a modern name for varied eleventh- and twelfth-century art. Monastic growth and pilgrimage supported major construction, sculpture and liturgical furnishings. Churches could organize a nave, aisles and apse, while large pilgrimage buildings added routes and chapels for visitors. Their scale should not define a modest parish. Painted surfaces, sculptured capitals and precious textiles could coexist with heavy masonry. [Julien Chapuis, Metropolitan Museum, Romanesque art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/romanesque-art).

Stone vaulting required substantial support and influenced wall thickness, openings and lighting. **Story inference:** the canon's compact barrel-vaulted church is a workable design, but Romanesque is not a rule that every roof must be stone or every opening a narrow slit. Check structural form building by building. Keep strong daylight at doors and windows, and allow varied construction budgets and repairs. [French Catholic heritage explanation of Romanesque churches](https://eglise.catholique.fr/accueil/sengager-dans-la-societe/patrimoine-et-art-sacre/372184-eglises-romanes/).

### Gothic belongs to the next generations

The coherent early Gothic program associated with Suger's work at [Saint-Denis](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Basilique+de+Saint-Denis%2C+France) dates to **1140–1144**. Large expanses of stained glass, skeletal support and the mature cathedral compositions familiar from later images belong beyond 1101. A single pointed arch is not enough to classify or date a building; assess the structural system and construction phase. [Metropolitan Museum, Gothic art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/gothic-art).

## What is current, ascendant, waning or later?

This table synthesizes the dated evidence above. “Ascendant” describes an observable wider development, not proof of immediate adoption in this valley.

| Area | Current in 1101 | Ascendant or developing | Waning, continuing, or later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sacred music | Chant, Office, oral learning and manuscript aids. | New teaching tools and notation; some early polyphony in documented centers. | Oral learning continues. Notre-Dame's elaborate repertory, mensural notation, *Ars nova* and the full familiar *Dies irae* are later. |
| Secular music | Portable instruments have regional archaeological support; ordinary songs need reconstruction. | The earliest surviving Occitan lyric tradition lies close to the period. | No evidence here shows local heroic storytelling or vernacular song disappearing. The mature troubadour canon is later. |
| Art | Painted religious space, manuscript illumination, embroidery and reused precious objects. | Monastic and pilgrimage patronage support ambitious work. | Romanesque art is active, not exhausted. Older materials and motifs continue through reuse. Isenheim is an intentional later reference. |
| Clothing | Layered garments, practical footwear and small fasteners; social differences in material and finish. | Changing elite silhouettes and broader textile exchange, with uneven dates. | Basic tunic forms persist. Elaborate later court costumes and fixed household liveries require separate evidence. |
| Architecture | Varied Romanesque construction beside older and repaired buildings. | Larger religious projects and increasingly elaborate decoration. | Older buildings can remain useful. Gothic is future; today's famous local monuments contain substantial later work. |

No dated local inventory, musical customary, secular tune collection or complete wardrobe for 1101 was found in this research. The result is a supported design range, not an exact reconstruction of each scene. Images may dress biblical figures conventionally; museum reconstructions test possibilities; buildings accumulate later campaigns. These limits should remain attached to the relevant reference when it enters an art or sound brief.
