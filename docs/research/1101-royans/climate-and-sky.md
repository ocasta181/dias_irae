# Climate and the sky in 1101

The story's damp, cold season is plausible for [Royans](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Royans%2C+France), at the western edge of the [Vercors](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Vercors%2C+France). It is a choice within the regional environment, rather than a recovered weather record for 1101. The strongest sky finding is more definite: **1101 supplies neither a red total lunar eclipse nor a solar eclipse visible at the representative local points checked for this report.** Its autumn lunar eclipses were extremely faint penumbral events.

This report covers late summer through winter 1101 and the wider [Isère](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Is%C3%A8re%2C+France), [Drôme](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Dr%C3%B4me%2C+France), and [Rhône](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Rh%C3%B4ne+river%2C+France) corridor. The [story outline](../../../story/story-arch.md) and [location dossiers](../../../story/locations.md) establish the fiction. They are not historical weather evidence.

## What the evidence can establish

The sources answer different questions. Tree rings are **natural proxies**: physical traces used to estimate an earlier climate variable. Their results depend on the trees, region, season, and statistical method. Medieval chronicles are **documentary reports**, with uneven coverage and possible errors in dates. Modern park descriptions establish the landscape and present climate mechanisms. Eclipse tables are **modern astronomical predictions**, not recovered eyewitness observations. Suggested scene details below are explicitly **story inference**. This report uses no ice core or other physical proxy to establish a local disaster or its cause in 1101.

No source opened for this report gives a continuous local weather series for Royans in 1093–1102. Thus, a recorded northern flood cannot establish local rain; a broad climate reconstruction cannot establish the temperature in a church. Absence of a local entry also cannot establish that the year was normal.

## The wider climate: variable within the medieval warm interval

1101 falls within the broad interval often called the Medieval Climate Anomaly, also called the Medieval Warm Period. The term describes long and uneven patterns. It does not mean that every season, region, or year was warm.

Büntgen and colleagues reconstructed April–June precipitation from oak rings and June–August temperature from high elevation conifers. Their study identifies relatively favorable conditions during medieval prosperity and less variation around 1000–1200 than during some earlier crises. However, the precipitation calibration region lies farther north, and the temperature record represents the [Austrian Alps](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Austrian+Alps). Neither record measures a Royans winter. The paper also warns against a simple chain from climate to historical events. [Büntgen et al., *2500 Years of European Climate Variability and Human Susceptibility*, main paper and supplement](https://www.climatology.uni-mainz.de/files/2018/08/Buentgen_2011_Science.pdf).

The Old World Drought Atlas gives a different view: tree rings reconstruct a summer soil moisture index across a large grid. It finds marked medieval dryness in north central [Europe](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Europe) and southern [Scandinavia](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Scandinavia), using 1000–1200 as its medieval comparison period. This does not contradict every report of wet medieval conditions. The two studies compare different regions, seasons, and variables. A soil moisture index is not rainfall in millimeters. [Cook et al., *Old World megadroughts and pluvials during the Common Era*](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/10023/7775/1/Wilson_2015_SA_Megadroughts_CC.pdf).

Winter needs its own evidence. Pfister and colleagues classified winter conditions from frost, frozen water, snow cover, and plant activity in historical documents. Their study spans western central Europe, including eastern [France](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=France). It contains cold episodes within the medieval warm interval. In particular, it identifies an eight week cold spell in winter 1099/1100 from nine contemporary sources in four countries. Those accounts do not establish the duration of snow or frozen rivers. This is a useful recent cold episode, not a measurement of Royans. [Pfister et al., *Winter air temperature variations in western Europe during the Early and High Middle Ages*, pp. 535–543](https://www.hist.unibe.ch/unibe/portal/fak_historisch/dga/hist/content/e11168/e52524/e69145/e186327/e1752851/pane1752905/e188661/50_Pfister-winterairtemp-98_ger.pdf).

For the story, the defensible conclusion is modest: a broadly favorable medieval climate can contain a wet harvest, a harsh winter, and local hunger. There is no need to place the whole setting in the later Little Ice Age to justify cold scenes.

## The local landscape and seasonal contrasts

The official Vercors park description shows why a single regional weather label is inadequate. Western and northwestern airflows meet the mountain barrier and bring substantial precipitation. The southern and southeastern sectors have stronger Mediterranean summer dryness. Higher ground is colder and more exposed to snow; slope direction also matters. The park contrasts lower [Saint-Jean-en-Royans](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Saint-Jean-en-Royans%2C+France) with the nearby, higher [forest of Lente](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=For%C3%AAt+de+Lente%2C+France). Its description of the north wind, or *bise*, associates it with cold, dry, often clear conditions; southerly winds can bring warmer, gusty, cloudy conditions. These are modern observations of regional mechanisms. Modern averages cannot be assigned to 1101. [Parc naturel régional du Vercors, *Le climat*](https://www.parc-du-vercors.fr/le-climat).

**Story inference:** use the relief to make journeys change in character. A traveler can leave a milder valley, enter a shaded gorge, and reach cold upland pasture. This is stronger than making the whole corridor uniformly wet and frozen. Low clouds and a damp church fit particular spells of weather. They need not persist without a break. A clear, cutting wind can be equally hostile and can expose the stars that cloud had concealed.

| Story season | Plausible scene choices; not documented 1101 weather |
|---|---|
| Late summer | Lower fields and higher fields need not ripen together. A storm can interrupt work, while a sheltered slope remains usable. Grain quality becomes visible during harvest and threshing. |
| Autumn | Rain can complicate drying, transport, and sowing. Mud can restrict carts before snow does. Stores become a material source of confidence or fear. |
| Winter | Short daylight, exposed paths, cold stone interiors, and reliance on stored supplies can narrow the characters' choices. Snow can obstruct upland travel while lower routes remain open. |

The agricultural details of tools, crops, and supply routes belong to the companion technology report. Here, the main climate point is the unequal exposure of fields, settlements, and roads.

The following records establish troubled years elsewhere. Their spatial limits are part of the finding.

Slavin's modern reassessment adds reports of drought in 1095, especially in northern France and the [Low Countries](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Low+Countries%2C+Europe). It also shows that hunger and mortality were not equally reported everywhere. Wet conditions in one year and drought in another must remain separate. His disease discussion notes the lack of diagnostic detail for the mortality of 1093/1094. Calling that whole episode ergot poisoning would exceed the evidence. [Slavin, *Crusaders in Crisis*, especially pp. 178–188](https://www.storre.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/30400/1/Slavin-ITMA-2010.pdf).

**Story inference:** memories of the hunger of 1095 are credible in a story set six years later. A character can recall scarcity, displacement, or a report from a distant monastery. A specific Royans family tragedy is fictional unless separately documented. Keep the experiences distinct: hunger from a failed harvest, poisoning from contaminated food, and infectious disease can coexist without being the same cause.

## Rye, wet weather, and ergot

Ergot is a plant disease caused here by *Claviceps purpurea*. The dark fungal bodies that replace infected grains are called **sclerotia**. The Max Rubner-Institut's modern guide describes infection of cereal flowers, especially unfertilized flowers with sufficient moisture. Rye is particularly susceptible because its flowers remain open for cross pollination; other cereals can also be affected. Some sclerotia remain in the field and produce spores in the next growing season. Others accompany harvested grain. This is modern biological evidence, not a medieval agronomic record. [Max Rubner-Institut, *Recommendations for action to minimise ergot and ergot alkaloids in cereals*, introduction](https://www.mri.bund.de/fileadmin/MRI/Institute/GE/241125_Handlungsempfehlungen_engl_bfrei.pdf).

The American Phytopathological Society explains that rain or wet soil supports production of the initial spores. Cool, wet conditions can delay pollination and extend the vulnerable period. Secondary spread can occur when insects or rain move infected floral secretions between flowers. Thus, **weather during flowering matters**. Autumn rain alone does not explain an infection that already took place earlier. [American Phytopathological Society, *Ergot of rye*, disease cycle and epidemiology](https://www.apsnet.org/edcenter/pdlessons/Pages/Ergot.aspx).

**Story inference:** the infection can become a crisis at harvest or during later consumption, when the damaged grain enters stores and food. A wet flowering period earlier in the year is a plausible background choice. It has not been documented locally for 1101. Grain from different fields or batches can carry different levels of contamination, which permits unequal illness within the same village.

Instant corruption of all crops following supernatural relic dust is an intentional break from this biological sequence. A prolonged wet autumn can strengthen the scene's mood and complicate storage and travel, but it need not be the moment when the fungus infected the grain. The companion provisions report addresses ingestion and processing.

## Astronomical events in 1101

**Calendar convention:** all precise eclipse and comet dates below use the **Julian calendar**. They are not modern Gregorian dates. The catalogue dates identify the date of greatest eclipse; a local eclipse can begin the previous evening. Year spans in the climate discussion retain the source's dating rather than converting day dates.

The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) catalogue lists four lunar eclipses in 1101, all **penumbral**: the Moon enters only Earth's faint outer shadow. None enters the dark central shadow needed for a partial or total lunar eclipse. Fred Espenak's revised solar catalogue lists two solar eclipses somewhere on Earth. A global classification does not establish local visibility. [NASA lunar catalogue, 1101–1200](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/LEcat5/LE1101-1200.html); [Espenak solar catalogue, 1101–1200](https://eclipsewise.com/solar/SEcatalog/SE1101-1200.html).

| Julian date in 1101 | Global event | Relevance to the representative Royans point |
|---|---|---|
| 15 April | Penumbral lunar; magnitude 0.4467 | Below the horizon during the event. |
| 30 April | Annular solar | Not listed as visible in the local calculation. |
| 14 May | Penumbral lunar; magnitude 0.6032 | Moon above the horizon; at most a subtle shading, near the usual detection threshold. Before the story's main season. |
| 9 October | Penumbral lunar; magnitude 0.1776 | Begins late on 8 October locally, reaches maximum after midnight; too faint for a normal visible eclipse scene. |
| 24 October | Total solar | Not listed as visible in either local calculation; no supported local daytime darkness. |
| 7 November | Penumbral lunar; magnitude 0.1732 | Moon above the horizon in the evening, but the shadow is too faint for a normal visible eclipse scene. |

A penumbral magnitude measures how deeply the Moon enters the outer shadow, not the fraction that turns black. Espenak's appearance guide places recognizable penumbral shading roughly above magnitude 0.6, with visibility depending on conditions and observation. The May event is marginal. The October and November events are much weaker. Their calculated occurrence cannot support a visibly red or bitten Moon. [Espenak, *Visual Appearance of Lunar Eclipses*](https://eclipsewise.com/lunar/LEhelp/LEappearance.html).

### Local calculation and its limits

The local results were generated with Fred Espenak and Chris O'Byrne's [Solar Eclipse Explorer](https://eclipsewise.com/solar/JSEX/JSEX-EU.html) and [Lunar Eclipse Explorer](https://eclipsewise.com/lunar/JLEX/JLEX-EU.html). Inputs were **45°03′ north, 5°20′ east, altitude 200 meters**, as a representative Royans point. A second solar check used **44°45′ north, 5°00′ east, 200 meters**, toward [Crest](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Crest%2C+France). Both solar searches for 1101–1200 begin with **31 May 1109, Julian**; neither includes a 1101 solar eclipse.

The calculators use **Besselian elements**, mathematical parameters describing eclipse geometry, and **delta T**, the estimated difference between uniform astronomical time and time based on Earth's rotation. The time zone input was zero, so output clock times are Universal Time, not medieval local clock readings. These are model predictions. Historical Earth rotation is uncertain; actual clouds and the local mountain horizon are not represented. The coordinates are sample points, not a survey of the fictional village or every location in the corridor. Results credit: *Eclipse Predictions by Fred Espenak and Chris O'Byrne (EclipseWise.com)*.

This is sufficient to reject an assumed Royans solar spectacle based only on the word “total” in the global October catalogue. It does not recover what any named villager saw.

## Earlier and later skies

Sigebert reports a moving fiery spear on **1 August 1093, Julian**, and a western comet in the first week of **October 1097, Julian**. These are recorded descriptions. A meteor is one possible modern explanation for the spear; the passage alone does not establish its nature or an impact. Such reports could supply remembered signs or travelers' tales, not verified local observations. [Sigebert, pp. 366–367](https://www.dmgh.de/mgh_ss_6/index.htm#page/366/mode/1up).

NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory great comet list places Halley's notable appearance in **1066**, with a first reported observation on **2 April 1066, Julian**. It places the great comet of **1106** first on **2 February 1106, Julian**. The first is an older memory in 1101; the second lies five years after the story. Neither should be moved into the campaign's autumn. [Jet Propulsion Laboratory, *Great Comets in History*](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/sb/great_comets.html).

For a possible later continuation, **5 April 1102, Julian**, offers a partial lunar eclipse globally, but the local calculation puts the start of the partial phase only about three degrees above the horizon, near dawn. The maximum is below the horizon. A ridge could hide the visible beginning. **17 September 1103, Julian**, supplies a total lunar eclipse with the Moon well above the representative horizon. Both dates are outside the current story. [Lunar Eclipse Explorer](https://eclipsewise.com/lunar/JLEX/JLEX-EU.html).

## Astrology and Christian signs

An astronomical event is a physical occurrence. An astrological or religious interpretation assigns meaning to it. These must be separated in the write-up and in character dialogue.

Bede's earlier *On the Nature of Things*, chapter 24, describes comets as signs of changes of rule, pestilence, war, winds, or heat. This is evidence for an inherited learned vocabulary of omens, available before 1101. It is not proof that every priest held these views or that a comet caused the predicted event. The linked edition includes later commentary; this claim comes from Bede's short base text. [Bede, *De natura rerum*, chapter 24](https://www.monumenta.ch/latein/text.php?domain=&hilite_id=&id=&inframe=1&lang=0&level=3&links=&rumpfid=Beda+Venerabilis%2C+De+Natura+Rerum%2C+++24&tabelle=Beda_Venerabilis).

Augustine supplies another inherited position. In *City of God*, book V, he rejects stellar determination of human choice while allowing natural effects such as the Sun's relation to seasons and the Moon's relation to tides. A Christian character can accept divine providence and reject a horoscope that removes human responsibility. Reading a disaster as a warning is not automatically the same as believing that birth stars fix a person's fate. [Augustine, *City of God*, book V, chapters 1–7](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120105.htm).

More elaborate Arabic and Greek astrological learning would expand in Latin during the twelfth century. Slavin notes Plato of Tivoli's Latin translation of Ptolemy's *Tetrabiblos* in **1138**, later than the story. That date is not a ban on all earlier astrology; it is a reason to avoid giving an ordinary local priest the complete later textbook culture without a specific educational history. [Slavin, p. 185](https://www.storre.stir.ac.uk/bitstream/1893/30400/1/Slavin-ITMA-2010.pdf).

**Story inference:** characters can disagree about the same sky. One calls a strange light a warning and demands penance; another warns against divination; a traveler repeats a remote report. A dramatic omen perceived through illness or fear can belong to the fiction even when the narrator does not certify it as a real 1101 eclipse.

## What is current, emerging, or later

**Current in 1101:** seasonal dependence, large contrasts between valley and upland conditions, recollection of recent scarcity, and older Christian debates about signs and fate. These are useful background conditions and intellectual resources, with the local evidence limits stated above.

**Emerging over the following century:** the wider Latin reception of translated astrological texts. Do not compress this process into an overnight change at 1100.

**Later:** the great comet of 1106, the more useful local lunar eclipse of 1103, and the later Little Ice Age as a broad climate period. None explains a specific scene in 1101. The evidence does not identify a distinct climate regime that suddenly waned in the campaign's year.

## Concrete use in the story

1. **Keep the damp atmosphere as a stated fictional season.** Vary it with clearer, colder spells, and let elevation and shade matter. This preserves the mood without claiming a documentary reconstruction.
2. **Make the blight's natural history sequential.** Flowering infection, damaged harvest, mixed stores, and later consumption support a slow discovery. Immediate corruption of mature fields remains supernatural.
3. **Make famine memory specific to characters.** The regional crisis of 1095 gives the memory a credible wider context. The fictional family, village losses, and local weather still need authorial choices.
4. **Keep real autumn 1101 eclipses visually quiet.** A conspicuous red Moon or daytime eclipse requires an invented phenomenon or another year. A faint predicted eclipse can still interest a learned observer without alarming a whole village.
5. **Use disagreement about signs.** Bede's comet vocabulary and Augustine's rejection of stellar fate supply different positions within Christian learning. Ordinary people need not share one uniform explanation.

The unresolved evidence is precise: there is no verified Royans weather account for 1101 here; no measured local flowering, harvest, snow, or river freezing dates; no confirmed Royans witness to the listed sky events; and no documented horoscope for the fictional characters. These gaps leave room for invention. They do not turn the invented details into historical fact.
