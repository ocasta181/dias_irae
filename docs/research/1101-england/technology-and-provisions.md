# Technology and provisions: England compared with the Royans in 1101

This supplement compares [England](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=England) with the story's [Royans](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Royans%2C+France), [Isère](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Is%C3%A8re%2C+France), [Drôme](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Dr%C3%B4me%2C+France), and [Rhône corridor](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Rh%C3%B4ne+Valley%2C+France) during **late summer through winter 1101**. The clearest differences concern crop patterns, beverage access, fisheries, and particular resource networks. The evidence does not support a national ranking of technological sophistication. Read alongside the [regional technology and provisions report](../1101-royans/technology-and-provisions.md); its precautions about later equipment and modern foods remain applicable.

## Crops, processing, and stores

English archaeology shows regional preferences. The Oxford *Feeding Medieval England* project finds oats and rye increasingly prominent in western lowlands, barley especially frequent in the [Fens](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=The+Fens%2C+England), and a strong wheat emphasis in its Central Zone, including the [Midlands](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Midlands%2C+England). These patterns were visible in **c.880–1030** and continued into its broad **c.1030–1220** phase. Wheat also occurred extensively elsewhere; these are preferences, not exclusive crop territories. The same research identifies increasing bulk cereal processing, mouldboard plough use, and systematic rotation. Older ards—implements that scratched rather than fully turned the soil—persisted alongside ploughs. A uniform three-field system cannot be assigned to every English village in 1101. [*Feeding Medieval England*, chapter 7](https://academic.oup.com/book/61548/chapter/537305187).

The rye-rich early-eleventh-century finds at [Colletière](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Colleti%C3%A8re%2C+Charavines%2C+France), discussed in the regional report, therefore provide a comparison with selected English districts, not proof of “French rye versus English wheat.” Neither dataset reconstructs the story region's crop ratios in 1101.

**Story inference.** Harvest scenes should separate food grain, seed grain, animal feed, and grain destined for dues or sale. Late summer and autumn can involve processing and storage; winter increasingly draws on stocks. No exact 1101 harvest calendar or household ration has been established. Estate grain records and concentrated burnt deposits are not complete menus.

## Ale, water, English vineyards, and imported wine

An earlier English witness makes ale an especially useful everyday reference: in **Ælfric's early-eleventh-century *Colloquy***, the pupil drinks ale when available and water otherwise, and says he cannot afford wine. This is a teaching dialogue with social assumptions, not a consumption survey. Its distinction between *ealu* and *win* is clear; modern words such as “beer” must not determine a period recipe. Do not give its ale a modern hopped-beer identity. Water drinking also directly defeats the claim that everyone needed alcohol because all water was unsafe. [Christine Fell, “Old English Beor,” 1975, p. 83](https://digital.library.leeds.ac.uk/132/1/LSE1975_pp76-95_Fell_article.pdf).

England nevertheless had vineyards. The Hull Domesday Project identifies them at **45 recorded places in 1086**, in southeastern England, held by Normans or great abbeys. It notes earlier documentary cultivation and expansion after 1066. These records establish wine production and privileged ownership, not nationwide access. Early-twelfth-century praise of English wine is **later testimony**, not a measurement of the 1101 vintage. [Hull Domesday Project: vineyards](https://www.domesdaybook.net/domesday-book/data-terminology/manors/vineyard).

Wine could also arrive by sea: the *Colloquy*'s imaginary overseas merchant lists it among imported goods. This supports familiarity with wine imports before 1101, while establishing neither quantities nor a particular port-to-estate route. The later commercial structures associated with royal control of [Gascony](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Gascony%2C+France) must retain their later dates. [Shane Bobrycki, 2024, section “A world of small merchants?”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/emed.12683).

**Comparison.** Ale can distinguish an English scene without making wine absent or universal in the story region. The regional report establishes a wider repertoire of wine and grain drinks, but does not measure their relative local consumption. No researched contrast justifies making cider standard in either setting.

## Marine fish and freshwater rights

Fish bones from **127 English archaeological assemblages** show a major increase in herring and cod around **1000**. More closely dated deposits place the shift in [London](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=London%2C+England) by **c.1050–1070**, and at inland [Eynsham Abbey](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Eynsham+Abbey%2C+Oxfordshire%2C+England) in the **late eleventh to early twelfth century**. Marine fish were becoming more important before the story; herring had also been used earlier. These remains cannot supply absolute catches or everyone's portion. The study's unambiguous written evidence for widespread cured-fish trade is **twelfth-century** evidence. Christian fasting and urban demand are explanations for expansion, not proof of one cause or one universal fasting menu. [Barrett, Locker, and Roberts, 2004, pp. 2417–2420](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/1317/1/barrettjh1.pdf).

This supports different provisioning possibilities in English coastal and urban scenes from an inland Royans household. It does not establish that marine fish were absent from the Rhône corridor, or that English river fisheries disappeared.

## Mills, dues, and access to resources

The **1086** entry for [Ombersley](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Ombersley%2C+Worcestershire%2C+England), held by the church of [Evesham](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Evesham%2C+England), records two mills, fisheries rendering **2,000 eels**, meadow, woodland, and a salt-pan at [Droitwich](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Droitwich%2C+Worcestershire%2C+England). Another estate entry assigns oxen to hauling stone to the church. These details connect agricultural labor, mills, fisheries, industrial resources, and construction. They also demonstrate institutional claims on production. Eel renders are dues, not a count of an ordinary person's meals. Nor should every asset be assumed unchanged fifteen years later. [Domesday entry transcriptions, based on Williams and Martin's edition](https://www.badseysociety.uk/directories-and-other-records/1086-domesday-book).

Watermills were already part of both economies. England's survey provides unusually systematic estate evidence; the availability of that record does not prove a technological advantage over the story region. A tenant's access to grain, a mill, a fishery, or woodland depended on particular holdings and obligations.

## Salt, fuel, and transport networks

Droitwich gives a concrete contrast with the Mediterranean saltworks considered in the regional report: **inland brine production**, rather than coastal production. The Worcestershire archaeological framework cites **972** salt-making vats and describes Droitwich as a major industrial center in **1086**, with fuel supply carefully controlled. It also identifies roads, river trade, and radiating saltways as parts of regional exchange. Its excavated boiling hearths date to the **fifth–early seventh centuries**; no salt-making features there have yet been securely dated to the **seventh/eighth–twelfth-century** interval. Documentary continuity therefore supports the industry, while the exact 1101 installation remains uncertain. [Worcestershire Research Framework: industry and trade](https://researchframeworks.org/worcs/worcestershire/early-medieval-research-framework/industry-and-trade/).

**Story inference.** An English supplier can draw salt from a documented inland industry, while the Royans supplier requires its own plausible route. Fuel, transport, rights, and labor can constrain supplies in either setting. Neither comparison warrants effortless access to salt, timber, iron, or imported luxuries for every household.

## Shared technology and practical scene choices

Hand tools, smithing, woodworking, textile production, watermills, mail, and mounted transport belong to the shared technological repertoire. The [English culture report](culture.md) dates major building campaigns and manuscript evidence; it does not make every house stone-built or every inhabitant literate. No supported English difference requires replacing the regional report's cautions about medicine, armor, or preservation.

**For the story:** grain dishes or bread, available vegetables and pulses, and household-specific animal or preserved foods form a reasonable scene framework. Wealth, dues, fasting observance, season, and access to markets change that framework. These are reconstruction choices, not a recovered national peasant menu. The supported ascendant developments are regional agricultural expansion and greater marine-fish use; no general technological decline is established. Exact 1101 crop quantities, recipes, imports, and household stocks remain unresolved.
